Niño 1+2 or Niño 3.4?

Niño 1+2 tracks the ocean off northern Peru and Ecuador: it drives heavy rain, debris flows and fishery shifts along the coast. Niño 3.4 tracks the central Pacific and defines El Niño globally.

Why does +1 °C matter?

A 1 °C warmer ocean evaporates more water and reshapes winds. In 1983 and 1998, anomalies of about +4 °C brought the worst rainfall of the 20th century to Tumbes and Piura.

Who issues official alerts?

Peru's multisector commission ENFEN issues the official statements and the coastal index (ICEN). This site is educational: always follow ENFEN, SENAMHI and INDECI.

04Climate projections

How will Peru's climate change by 2050?

Annual mean temperature and relative rainfall change from 1960 to 2049 in two high-resolution CMIP6 climate models, compared with the 1981–2010 baseline.

Warming stripes

Each stripe is one year: annual mean temperature compared with the 1981–2010 average. Years to the right of “Today” are model projections.

Average of the models Range between models 1981–2010 baseline
Show decade averages as a table

Rainfall is shown as a relative change because coarse model grids cannot reproduce absolute rainfall in Peru's hyper-arid coast. Projections are not forecasts: they show the long-term trend under a high-emissions pathway (close to RCP8.5), not the weather of a given year. Models: EC-Earth3P-HR and MRI-AGCM3-2-S (CMIP6 HighResMIP), bias corrected by Open-Meteo. Built by an automated pipeline on GitHub Actions.
